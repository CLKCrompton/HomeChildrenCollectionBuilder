# Historical Context Essay: The Work Being Done

	Much work was involved with the Home Children, in more than one way. There was work involved on the part of adults involved in bringing the Home Children from the United Kingdom to Canada as well as in attempting to gain support for their operations and find placements for the children they brought. There is, of course, also the matter of the work that was performed by the children themselves. 

	To start, it is worthwhile to analyze the adults, the work they did to maintain the operation of this immigration for the many years during which this movement of children occurred, and their motivations for doing so. 

Firstly, there was certainly an element of what they perceived as genuinely good intentions, though modern readers are likely to view their intentions as having been heavily influenced by the biases they held. 

Bagnell (2001) describes religious compassion as being a motivating factor behind this work, and also details how commentary in the aftermath of Doyle’s report on their condition carried an undertone that the children ought to be grateful for the Canadians who rescued them from their poor circumstances. A key part of the arguments that were being made in favour of this immigration scheme was that the children would be taught some sort of beneficial trade and thus able to become productive members of society once they became adults (Bagnell, 2001; Doyle, 1875). Even Andrew Doyle, whose report on the condition of the children condemned very nearly everything about the entire operation, balanced his critique by praising the good intentions of the adults (1875). He even goes so far as to argue that consideration should be given to the hardships that they have had to endure in undergoing this work as well as emphasizing that the work done by Annie Macpherson and Maria Rye has “undoubtedly” produced much good (Doyle, 1875, p. 4). So much good, Doyle argues, that it would be impossible to speak too highly of it (1875, p. 16). 

Furthermore, the immigration of the Home Children was not only the work of individuals like Maria Rye or Annie Macpherson. It was also facilitated by myriad government efforts. Bagnell (2001) describes monetary bonuses provided by the Ontario government for each child brought over the age of 12, reduced fares for travel by ship, and even free train travel from Quebec to Niagra-on-the-Lake (p. 34). 

	That being said, it is also worth pointing out that, regardless of their intentions or reasons, there are indications that many of the parents of the Home Children either did not know that their child was going to be sent to Canada or else felt like they had no choice in the matter (Corbett, 2002; Veteran Affairs Canada, 2025). This reality weakens the good intentions behind the adults running this operation because this brings questions of unethical behaviour into play. Judgemental and borderline condescending attitudes towards the children at least partially connected to the statuses of their parents and how that reflected on the children themselves (see for example Bagnell, 2001) combined with the above-mentioned questionable ethics in parental consent (or lack thereof) raise the question of whether at least some of the time decisions were made to transport children with the intention of freeing them from what may have been considered poor parental influence. Evidence does suggest that the labour or trade they were taught was considered good for the children and their development into productive members of society (Bagnell, 2001; Doyle, 1875). 

In addition, whatever good intentions may have been behind all the work that went into the functioning of the program, they do not outweigh or make up for the suffering that many of these children endured as a result of their immigration and subsequent labour.

To start, the majority of these children were brought to Canada primarily not to be adopted into loving families or to be housed in locations that were less crowded, but for the sake of cheap (often agricultural) labour. Bagnell (2001) lists multiple examples, such as the 100 boys brought by Annie Macpherson in 1870 with most of them being housed overnight and then immediately sent out to the countryside to work on farms (p. 28) or the first 50 children brought by Maria Rye who initially had some trouble being placed but were in time all settled somewhere to work (p. 33). Bagnell also describes Annie Macpherson as going out to speak to try to raise support for the work being done, with a key element of her argument being not charitable aims but the audience’s need for reliable and cheap labour (a need that the children she brought to Canada could fulfil) – a strategy successful enough that many came directly to her after she had spoken to request child labourers (2001, p. 28). 

And, unfortunately, as described by both Bagnell (2001) and Doyle (1875) as well as by Corbett (2002), it was not unusual for these labouring children to endure abuse after having been placed. Though intended efforts to keep track of how the children were getting on are discussed, it is clear that the attention given to this area of the operation was insufficient: children placed on farms that were more difficult to reach may not have been visited at all by anyone despite that children ought to have been checked up on, the farmers themselves were not likely to report their own mistreatment of the children under their care, and religious ministers ostensibly in charge of checking in on children were described as being unlikely to have more than a passing familiarity with many of the Home Children (Bagnell, 2001; Corbett, 2002; Veterans Affairs Canada, 2025). Some of these children even died as a result of the labour they performed and the way they were treated or punished for infractions; Corbett (2002) describes one such example. 

It is true that there are testimonials of Home Children and their experiences that are generally positive (see Corbett, 2002). However, it is worth being cautious about the potential for survivorship bias, which is defined by Eldridge and Britannica Editors (2024) as an error in logic that occurs when attention is given only to those who survived and thus leads to erroneous conclusions. The Home Children who died, for instance, were and are not able to give their accounts of their experiences; it is likely that their opinions would have differed significantly from Home Children who recount their experiences more positively. 

Even adoption was not necessarily a boon for the Home Children, for some recognized the risk of parents getting labour without having to pay for it (Corbett, 2002). There are also accounts of the children having difficulties in being paid what they were owed (Bagnell, 2001; Corbett, 2002) or being neglected such that the organizations that had placed them ended up losing track of them (Doyle, 1875). 

	The children themselves were also not safe from criticism, even from those who sought to argue against this practice. Bagnell (2001) describes critiques used in attempts to put an end to this importation of child labour, among them the argument that the children’s presence bringing down wages as well as an argument that such children were more prone to committing crimes. As put by Bagnell, the scheme was denounced and the children vilified (2001, p. 77). The children in some cases were so looked down on that some went so far as to wish that the ships carrying these children sunk on the voyage (Corbett, 2002). Criticism of the quality of children brought by these efforts became common enough in the time period this migration of children was occurring that it was even referenced in Anne of Green Gables (Montgomery, 2018). 

Doyle’s report was also targeted and criticised by his opponents and supporters of the Home Children immigration work. Bagnell (2001) details the way that Doyle’s report was criticized for unfairness (and even accused of having religious bias backing it in one case, for Doyle was a Roman Catholic and both Macpherson and Rye were Protestants), and also comments on how the men and women in Canada who were actually the ones taking these children on received no criticism for their role in the operation. 

	There is no denying that the Home Children had a significant impact on the development of Canada, particularly when it came to the sheer number of these children (more than 100,000) being sent to work on farms across Canada (Bagnell, 2001; Corbett, 2002; Doyle, 1875; Veteran Affairs Canada, 2025). British Home Children did quite a lot of work: much of it was agricultural, but many of the girls sent ended up doing domestic labour in addition to helping out in the fields (Bagnell, 2001; Corbett, 2002; Doyle, 1875; Veteran Affairs Canada, 2025). Assuming they survived the experience, many went on to contribute to Canadian society in other ways: many, for example, served and died fighting for Canada in both the First and Second World War (Veteran Affairs Canada, 2025). It has even been suggested that as much as 10% of Canada’s current population may descend from the Home Children that had been brought here (Veteran Affairs Canada, 2025). 

	In summary, though many of the adults (biased though they may have been) had at least some good intentions in bringing children to Canada, it is difficult to argue that the immigration of the Home Children was a wholly good thing given how much harm and mistreatment the Home Children had to endure as well as the questionable or neglectful conduct of the adults who should have been responsible for ensuring their well-being. It is important that this history, the Home Children, and their stories not be forgotten.
