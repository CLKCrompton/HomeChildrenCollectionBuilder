## Thomas J. Barnardo
Thomas John Barnardo was an Irish Christian philanthropist and founder of the Barnardo’s Homes for Children. He was born in Dublin, Ireland in 1845 and moved to London in 1866 where he began his interest in missionary work. He first began to study to become a medical missionary in China, however never finished his degree and his life’s mission was changed after seeing the stark reality of the city’s East End poverty. In the late 1860s, Barnardo established a Ragged School at Hope Place and began providing free basic education to poor children. However at the same time, there was a cholera outbreak increasing the poverty and homelessness of children on the streets of London. A pivotal moment during this time for Barnarbo was an encounter with a young boy named Jim Jarvis where the young boy showed him where he and other kids would sleep on rooftops and in gutters of the city for warmth. Barnardo’s disbelief increased his desire to aid child homelessness in which he famously vowed “_No Destitute Child Ever Turned Away_”.  

In 1870, Barnardo opened his first Home for Working and Destitute Boys at 18 Stepney Causeway in East London. After his marriage to Sara Louise Elmslie  in 1873, they opened a Girl’s Village in Barkinside that looked like cottage homes where young girls would learn domestic work. By the turn of the century, the Girl’s Village had grown to accommodate 1500 girls. While Barnardo was praised for his missionary work, he also had several controversies throughout his career. Some include legal precedents where Barnardo was accused of kidnapping children such as the famous “Barnardo vs McHugh” case in 1891. There was also criticism for staged and manipulated photographs of before-and-afters of children for fundraisers. Most notably and one of the most damaging aspects of his work included his affairs in child emigration where he sent 30,000 children to Canada, Australia and South Africa. Many of these children were misled into fresh starts only to face hardships. The children were used for cheap, institutionalized farm labour or domestic servants and many suffered abuse and loss of cultural and family identity. 

After his death in 1905, Barnardo’s has continued to evolve into a supporting charity for young people and families across the UK.
