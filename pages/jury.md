## Alfred F. Jury
Alfred Fredman Jury was born January 31st, 1848 in Maidstone, England. He apprenticed as a tailor and in 1868 became a journeyman, joining the Amalgamated Society of Tailors. He rose rapidly through the ranks and became the president of his local branch and a representative to the Maidstone Trades Council.

He immigrated to Canada with his wife, Emma Hart, in 1873 where he settled in Toronto after living four months in Ottawa. There he would again become distinguished in labour circles, rising through the ranks as a member of the Tailors Operatives Union at the Toronto Trades Assembly. He would serve as the financial secretary, vice-president, president, and treasurer over the course of his time there.

In 1898, writing of their contemporary Jury, the International Brotherhood of Bookbinders said of his activity in the Trades Assembly that, “… none was more active than Alf. Jury …” (International Brotherhood of Bookbinders, 1898, p. 33). And it was only by virtue of his entering business himself in the 1880s that Jury was required to leave his position at the Trades Assembly, all members being required to be wage-earners.

He did not, however, stop championing the causes of labour after his exit. He was involved in myriad campaigns throughout the latter half of the 19th century, including as a leading voice in labour’s ultimately successful demands for changes to Ontario’s Master and Servant Act, decriminalizing breach-of-contract provisions and changes to the federal criminal law amendment act that attempted to limit union activities during strikes.

In 1887, he ran on a Labour ticket in the federal election for Toronto East after Labour’s prominence had begun to wane, but was ultimately defeated by Tory member John Small.

Even as he was no longer a participating member in trade union activities, he continued to advise young workers, that “any of the boys requiring a service done, and which service could be furthered by Alf. Jury, it was only necessary to drop into “Alf’s” tailor shop and make known his request; and such meeting with Alf’s approval down went the job, and Alf. was off to do what lay in his power.” (International Brotherhood of Bookbinders, 1898, p. 33).

He was described as “an effective public speaker and an old campaigner” (Morgan, 1912, p.595) and published a series of articles in the _Globe_ on labour and class issues.

He was appointed to the Labour government’s royal commission on the prison and reformatory system in 1890. In 1896, he was made dominion emigrant agent in Liverpool. He held the position until his death in 1916.

Jury was a leading figure of the Canadian labour movement in the 1870s.
